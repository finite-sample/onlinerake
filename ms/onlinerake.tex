\begin{abstract}
Raking adjusts survey weights so that weighted shares of demographic groups match population targets. The standard algorithm, iterative proportional fitting (IPF), needs every row seen so far, so keeping weights current on a stream means storing the stream and refitting as it grows. We give a raking method whose state is a fixed set of running totals. Each arriving row receives the weight that minimizes its raking distance from its design weight plus a penalty on the running gap between weighted and target margins; earlier weights stay fixed. This one-dimensional problem has a closed-form solution through the Wright omega function, so each arrival costs $O(p)$ time for $p$ margins and the method keeps $p+4$ numbers no matter how long the stream runs. The same objective minimized over all rows at once is entropy balancing with a ridge penalty, which tends to IPF as the penalty grows. In simulations across six scenarios (50 replications each), the error of a weighted outcome mean at the end of the stream is no more than 27\% above full-data IPF when targets are moderate, and 2\% below it in one scenario, while storing 8 to 12 numbers instead of 2,500 to 450,000. During the stream, it tracks the margins more closely than IPF refit every 1,000 rows or at each doubling of the stream in five of six scenarios, usually at a fraction of the compute. It does worse with extreme targets and with 50 margins (bias 11\% and 52\% above IPF), and its estimates depend on arrival order. When the rows can be stored, periodic IPF remains the better choice.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Survey weights are often adjusted so that the weighted sample matches known population shares: the weighted share of women should equal the census share of women, and likewise for age groups, regions, and education levels. When only the marginal shares are known, the standard adjustment is \emph{raking}, computed by iterative proportional fitting \citep[IPF;][]{Deming1940}. IPF takes each characteristic in turn and rescales the weights so that characteristic's weighted share matches its target, and cycles until all shares match.

IPF needs every row each time it runs. That is no problem for a finished survey, but many samples arrive as streams: online panels, rolling surveys, app telemetry, river samples. Weighted estimates are wanted while data arrive, and keeping weights current means storing every row and refitting as the sample grows. Refitting is affordable at survey scale: one full IPF fit over 50,000 rows and 8 margins took about 0.3 seconds in our runs. The binding constraints are elsewhere. Sometimes rows cannot be kept, because they contain personal data that should not be retained or because the volume is too large. Sometimes weighting happens on a device or in a pipeline with a fixed memory budget. And the cost of refitting grows with the stream: in our measurements, refitting every 1,000 arrivals costs about 42 microseconds per arrival at 10,000 rows and 5,685 at a million (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

This paper asks what raking is possible when all one can keep is a fixed set of running totals, and what that costs in accuracy. Our answer has three parts. First, we write raking as a penalized problem (Section~\ref{sec:objective}): weights should stay close to their design weights in the raking distance, and the running totals of weighted deviations from the targets should be small. Second, holding earlier weights fixed, the best weight for an arriving row has a closed form (Section~\ref{sec:update}). The update needs only the running totals, costs $O(p)$, and has one tuning parameter, the penalty strength, which says how much weight variability to accept to close the gap to the targets. Third, the same objective over all rows has an exact solution through a $p$-dimensional dual (Section~\ref{sec:batch}), which isolates the cost of not storing the data.

The experiments (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) compare the streaming method with full-data IPF, with IPF refit periodically on the stored stream, and with the earlier online methods in the \texttt{onlinerake} package. Section~\ref{sec:superseded} explains how this paper differs from its earlier version.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Raking as calibration}

Let row $i$ have a vector $x_i \in \{0,1\}^p$ of group indicators (for example $x_{i1}=1$ for women and $x_{i2}=1$ for people under 30) and a design weight $d_i > 0$, which is one when there is no sampling design. Let $t \in (0,1)^p$ be the target shares. The weighted share of group $j$ under weights $w$ is
\begin{equation}
  m_j(w) = \frac{\sum_i w_i x_{ij}}{\sum_i w_i}.
  \label{eq:margins}
\end{equation}
Usually many weight vectors give $m(w) = t$. Calibration \citep{Deville1992} picks the one closest to the design weights, where ``closest'' is set by a distance function. With the distance
\begin{equation}
  D(w, d) = w \log(w/d) - w + d,
  \label{eq:distance}
\end{equation}
summed over rows, calibration on group margins is raking \citep{Deville1992}; IPF computes it \citep{Csiszar1975}. Other distances give other estimators; the chi-square distance gives the generalized regression estimator. Entropy balancing \citep{Hainmueller2012} solves the raking problem through its dual: the optimal weights are an exponential tilt of the design weights, $w_i = d_i \exp(-\lambda \cdot (x_i - t))$, and the $p$ multipliers $\lambda$ are found by convex optimization.

\subsection{Penalizing instead of constraining}

Exact constraints are not the only option. \citet{Bardsley1984} proposed replacing the requirement that weighted totals equal their targets with a quadratic penalty on the difference, which trades a little bias for less variable weights. That idea is what makes a streaming version possible, because a penalty, unlike a constraint, can be partly satisfied by each arriving row.

\section{Streaming penalized raking}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Objective}
\label{sec:objective}

Write $\delta_i = x_i - t$ for how far row $i$ sits from the targets, and
\[
  r(w) = \sum_i w_i \delta_i
\]
for the running totals of weighted deviations. Since $r(w) = W\,(m(w) - t)$ with $W = \sum_i w_i$, the margins equal the targets exactly when $r = 0$. We minimize
\begin{equation}
  F(w) = \sum_i D(w_i, d_i) + \frac{c}{2}\, \lVert r(w) \rVert^2 .
  \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
The first term keeps weights near their design weights; the second pulls the margins toward the targets, with calibration strength $c > 0$ setting the exchange rate. $F$ is strictly convex. As $c$ grows, its minimizer approaches the raking solution whenever the targets are attainable (Section~\ref{sec:batch}).

\subsection{The arrival update}
\label{sec:update}

When row $i$ arrives, all earlier weights are fixed and summarized by the running totals $r_0$ accumulated so far. The only choice left is $w_i$, which should minimize
\[
  f(w) = D(w, d_i) + \frac{c}{2} \lVert r_0 + w\,\delta_i \rVert^2 .
\]
Its derivative is
\[
  f'(w) = \log(w/d_i) + c\,\delta_i \cdot r_0 + c\,q\,w, \qquad q = \lVert \delta_i \rVert^2 .
\]
Setting $f'(w)=0$ and substituting $u = c\,q\,w$ gives
\[
  u + \log u = \log(c\,q\,d_i) - c\,\delta_i \cdot r_0 .
\]
The solution of $u + \log u = y$ is the Wright omega function $\omega(y)$ \citep{Corless2002}, a standard special function available in SciPy. So
\begin{equation}
  w_i = \frac{\omega\!\left(\log(c\,q\,d_i) - c\,\delta_i \cdot r_0\right)}{c\,q},
  \label{eq:update}
\end{equation}
and $w_i = d_i$ when $q = 0$, that is, when the row sits exactly on the targets. Because $f$ is convex, clipping $w_i$ to bounds $[\varepsilon, M]$ gives the bounded minimizer; we use $[0.001, 100]$. The row then joins the running totals: $r \leftarrow r_0 + w_i \delta_i$.

The update reads naturally. If the row's deviation points against the current gap ($\delta_i \cdot r_0 < 0$, for example a man arriving when men are under-represented), the argument of $\omega$ is large and the row is weighted up; if it points with the gap, it is weighted down. The strength $c$ sets how hard each row pushes. The optimality condition can also be written $w_i = d_i \exp(-c\, r \cdot \delta_i)$ with $r$ the totals after the update: the same exponential tilt as entropy balancing, with the running quantity $c\,r$ in place of the multipliers.

\paragraph{Cost and state.} Each arrival needs two inner products of length $p$ and one evaluation of $\omega$: $O(p)$ time, independent of how many rows came before. The state is $r$ ($p$ numbers), the total weight, the sum of squared weights (for the effective sample size), and the count. Margins are $t + r/W$ at any moment. To estimate the mean of an outcome $y$, the state also keeps $\sum_i w_i y_i$; the estimate is that sum divided by $W$. With one outcome, the state is $p+4$ numbers and no rows are stored.

\subsection{The batch counterpart}
\label{sec:batch}

With all rows available, the minimizer of \eqref{eq:objective} satisfies $w_i = d_i \exp(-\lambda \cdot \delta_i)$ with $\lambda = c\,r(w)$, so it is found from the $p$-dimensional convex dual
\[
  G(\lambda) = \sum_i d_i \exp(-\lambda \cdot \delta_i) + \frac{\lVert \lambda \rVert^2}{2c},
\]
which is the entropy balancing dual plus a ridge penalty on the multipliers. We solve it with Newton's method using the analytic gradient and Hessian. As $c \to \infty$ the ridge term vanishes and the solution is the raking solution: at $c = 10^6$ it matches IPF weights to $10^{-6}$ relative error in the package's tests. Comparing this batch solution with the single pass at the same $c$ isolates what is lost by never revisiting a weight.

\subsection{What the earlier version of this paper proposed}
\label{sec:superseded}

An earlier version of this paper (March 2026) also adjusted only the arriving row's weight, but by a fixed number of gradient steps with a fixed step size on the squared margin error. The gradient with respect to the arriving weight is $(1/W)\sum_j (m_j - t_j)(x_j - m_j)$. After an arrival the margin error is of order $1/t$ and $W$ is of order $t$, so the gradient shrinks like $1/t^2$ and, with a fixed step, the new weight barely moves from one. As a result that update leaves the margins essentially at their unweighted values; the package's test suite now checks this (the final margin error stays within 2\% of the unweighted error on a 3,000-row stream). The experiments in that version had run a different algorithm that re-optimizes every stored weight at each arrival, which we report below as ``all-weights SGD.'' The present method replaces both.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}

\paragraph{Scenarios.} Each replication draws binary group indicators independently, row by row. \emph{Standard}: $n = 3{,}000$ rows, $p = 8$ margins, sample shares between 0.3 and 0.7, targets within 0.15 of them. \emph{Large}: the same with $n = 50{,}000$. \emph{Hard targets}: sample shares between 0.4 and 0.6 and targets of 0.08 or 0.92. \emph{Many margins}: as Standard with $p = 50$. \emph{Drift}: $n = 5{,}000$, with sample shares moving linearly to their complements over the stream and targets of 0.5. \emph{Small}: $n = 500$, $p = 4$. There are 50 replications per scenario.

\paragraph{Outcome and truth.} Each row has an outcome $y = 1 + \beta \cdot x + x_1 x_2 + \epsilon$ with $\beta_j \sim U(-1,1)$ and $\epsilon \sim N(0,1)$. In the population, groups are independent with shares $t$, so the true mean is $1 + \beta \cdot t + t_1 t_2$. The interaction term matters: a linear outcome would be estimated without bias by any weights that hit the margins, while the interaction rewards weights that also keep the groups independent, as IPF's product-form weights do.

\paragraph{Methods.} \emph{IPF}: full-data raking at the end of the stream. \emph{Stream}: the update of Section~\ref{sec:update} with $c \in \{0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1\}$; $c = 0.3$ is the package default and the one reported in the tables. \emph{Batch}: the minimizer of \eqref{eq:objective} at the same $c$. \emph{Periodic IPF}: IPF refit on all stored rows every 100 or 1,000 rows, or whenever the stream doubles in length; between refits, a new row gets the product of the last fit's per-group factors, which is the weight IPF would have given it, and the first refits come at 1, 2, 4, \dots\ rows so the warm-up period is covered. \emph{All-weights SGD} and \emph{MWU}: the package's earlier online classes, which store every row and re-optimize all weights at each arrival (run only for $n \le 5{,}000$ because their cost grows with $n^2$).

\paragraph{Metrics.} At the end of the stream: absolute bias of the weighted outcome mean, averaged over replications, and effective sample size $\text{ESS} = (\sum w)^2 / \sum w^2$ as a fraction of $n$. During the stream: mean absolute margin error averaged over 20 checkpoints (every 5\% of the stream). Cost: wall-clock microseconds per arrival, including every refit for periodic IPF. Memory: numbers retained.

The timings compare a Python per-row loop (the stream) with vectorized NumPy refits (IPF), on one machine whose load varied between runs; absolute times differed by a factor of two to four across runs for every method. We therefore read the timings as ratios within a run.

Table~\ref{tab:final} reports how much accuracy fixed memory costs. With moderate targets, the stream's bias is close to full-data IPF: 0.017 against 0.015 in Standard, 0.014 against 0.014 under Drift, 0.039 against 0.040 in Small, and 0.004 against 0.003 in Large, where both are small. Two scenarios show the limits. With extreme targets, the stream's bias is 0.142 against IPF's 0.128, and with 50 margins it is 0.038 against 0.025. The batch solution at the same $c$ matches or beats IPF in both (0.116 and 0.025), so the gap comes from never revisiting weights, not from the penalty. The all-weights SGD class has higher bias than the stream in all five scenarios where it ran, while storing every row.

\subsection{Arrival order}

Because earlier weights are never revisited, the stream's estimate depends on the order in which rows arrive. Reshuffling the same 3,000 rows ten times in each of 20 Standard replications, the estimate of the outcome mean varies with a standard deviation of 0.0086. The sampling variation of the same estimator across replications is 0.020. Order therefore adds noise equal to about two-fifths of the sampling noise, which matters for reporting uncertainty: a confidence interval that ignores it is too narrow.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{When to use what.} If the rows can be stored and the stream is of survey size, refit IPF periodically: it is exact at each refit, has the highest effective sample size, and its cost is small at these sizes. Use the streaming method when the rows cannot or should not be stored, when memory is fixed, or when the stream is long enough that refitting dominates the cost. Choose $c$ by Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}: around 0.3 for moderate targets, larger when targets are far from the sample.

\paragraph{Why a single pass works as well as it does.} Each arrival sees the current gap $r$ and leans against it, so under-represented groups are pulled up by the rows that arrive later. Early weights are set when the gap is noisy and are never corrected, which is why the effective sample size falls and why order matters. With many margins, each arrival can correct only one direction of a high-dimensional gap, and the single pass falls further behind the batch solution.

\paragraph{Limitations.} We have no convergence guarantee for the single pass; the evidence is the simulations. The scenarios draw groups independently; correlated groups, continuous calibration variables (which the package supports as mean targets), and adversarial orderings are untested. Drift is handled only in the sense that the weights follow the stream; there is no forgetting, so a stream whose population changes will be weighted toward its average. Uncertainty estimates for the streaming estimator, including the order component, remain to be developed.

\section{Conclusion}

Raking needs the data, but the streaming version needs only $p+4$ running totals. Each arriving row receives the weight that best trades closeness to its design weight against the running gap to the targets, in closed form. In simulations it comes close to full-data IPF when targets are moderate, tracks the margins during the stream better than IPF refit at similar cost, and keeps a constant cost per arrival as the stream grows. It falls short with extreme targets and many margins, and its estimates depend on arrival order. Where rows can be stored, periodic IPF remains the better tool.
